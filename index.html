Overview

Compare David’s and Ryan’s solve times, consistency, and recorded wins for Easy, Medium, and Hard puzzles. The charts describe recorded results; they don’t explain why performance changed.

The report includes recorded solves from December 01, 2025 through October 05, 2026. Missing times are excluded. Solve times are shown in minutes. The median represents a typical solve time and is less affected by very slow solves than the average. Table values are rounded for display; changes are calculated from unrounded medians.

Hard times above 20 minutes are treated as 20 minutes in every solve-time analysis and in the data table. This cap reduces the measured spread and can make different Hard times appear equal. A capped time doesn’t necessarily mean the puzzle was completed. Win rates use the winners recorded in the sheet, not the capped times.

The current month’s summaries use only the results recorded so far. Comparisons with a full month can change as more results are added.

8. Is relative Hard performance changing?

The daily gap shows who took longer on a shared puzzle. This analysis asks a different question: has David’s time relative to Ryan’s changed over the full record? It uses only Hard puzzles with a positive recorded time for both players, capped at 20 minutes each.

The estimated trend points toward David taking longer relative to Ryan, but the evidence does not establish a sustained decline.

The test includes 306 shared Hard puzzles from December 01, 2025 through October 04, 2026. Days without both times are excluded.

Table 8. Full-record relative-time trend
Primary result Estimate
Change in David/Ryan time ratio per 30 days +1.84%
95% confidence interval -1.12% to +4.89%
Two-sided p-value 0.225

For day \(d\), let \(D_d\) and \(R_d\) be David’s and Ryan’s recorded times in seconds. The outcome is the log of the capped time ratio:

\[ y_d = \ln\!\left(\frac{\min(D_d,1200)}{\min(R_d,1200)}\right). \]

The test fits a straight-line trend in this log ratio over calendar time. A positive slope means David’s relative time increased; a negative slope means it decreased. The reported percentage is \(100(e^{\beta}-1)\), where \(\beta\) is the change in log ratio per 30 days. It is a proportional change in the time ratio, not a change in win probability.

A ratio of 1 means equal capped times. Above 1 means David took longer; below 1 means Ryan took longer. The black line is the fitted trend. Its shaded band is a pointwise 95% confidence interval for the fitted mean log ratio, converted back to ratios. It is not a prediction interval for the next puzzle. The monthly points are geometric means of the individual puzzle ratios, not ratios of the players’ separate monthly medians.

The confidence interval includes improvement, no change, and deterioration. The result is inconclusive; it does not establish that performance stayed unchanged.

The p-value is not the probability that David is getting worse or that there is no trend. It describes how unusual an estimated slope this far from zero would be if the no-trend hypothesis and model assumptions held.

Methods, assumptions, and data checks

The model fits \(y_d = \alpha + \beta t_d + \epsilon_d\), where \(t_d\) is calendar days since the first shared puzzle, divided by 30. The null hypothesis is \(\beta = 0\); the test is two-sided. Confidence intervals use a normal reference distribution, so the inference is approximate and depends on the model’s assumptions.

Newey-West uncertainty estimates allow unequal residual variation and short-term dependence. The main test uses Bartlett weights, a seven-calendar-day lag window, and an \(n/(n-2)\) finite-sample correction. Missing dates contribute zero score vectors to the covariance calculation, not invented solve times or fitted observations. The win-odds model uses the same calendar-aware covariance method.

The bootstrap uses 10,000 circular residual resamples, blocks of seven successive shared games, seed 20261005, and a basic 95% interval. Calendar dates stay fixed. It assumes the residual process is approximately stationary around the fitted trend. R’s random-number generator differs from the attachment’s Python implementation, so bootstrap endpoints can differ slightly even with the same seed.

Comparing two times for the same puzzle reduces the effect of shared difficulty if it changes both times proportionally. Puzzle styles can still favor one player, and capping compresses large differences. Relative results cannot distinguish Ryan improving from David declining. These are retrospective analyses, not preregistered tests; repeated checks and selected date windows can give misleadingly small p-values.
